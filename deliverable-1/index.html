Fish, “Ball,” and the Limits of Reading

Scroll, and watch the color come back.

01The Blackboard

When Stanley Fish wrote a list of names on a blackboard and told his class it was a religious poem, his students didn’t hesitate. They found symbols, hidden layers, even a link between “Jacobs” and Jacob’s ladder (324). In “How to Recognize a Poem When You See One,” Fish uses that moment to argue that meaning doesn’t sit inside a text waiting to be discovered. Readers produce it, using strategies they share with the communities they belong to. This doesn’t mean any reading is as good as any other. It means that how we read is learned, and that what we call “the text” is already shaped by those habits. Fish calls these shared habits “interpretive communities”: groups of readers who have been taught what to look out for while reading, usually without noticing (335).

02A Red Ball

I got to test Fish’s claim when I watched “Ball,” a short film by John Baxa. An old man sits in a black-and-white world until a red ball appears. As he chases it, he grows younger, and color returns to the sky, the ground, and the trees. I read the film as a story about youth being a mindset, not an age. But when I look back at how I got to that reading, I see my own expectations and experiences at work as much as the film. My experience mostly supports Fish, but it also shows that a text plays a bigger role in shaping meaning than Fish gives it credit for; it doesn’t dictate a meaning, but it sets the limits of what a reader can choose.

03Looking for Meaning

Watching the film, I came in already looking for information that fit my schema of something I would watch in an English class. Throughout high school, everything we watched had some meaning or arguable interpretation, and that is the mindset I watched the video with. That means I watched the video in the same way that Fish’s students read the poem. They were already looking for meaning, not trying to make sense of what it was at first. Fish explains this when he writes that “the assumption that there is a central insight is itself poetry-specific, and presided over its own realization” (326). For me, that kind of attention meant that nothing in the film was just there. When the film opened in black and white, I did not see it as a style choice. I saw it as a symbol for the way the man is living: quiet, reserved, and lonely. When the trees in the background looked withered and dull, like an oak tree in the middle of winter, I immediately connected it to old age. Because I expected meaning, I found it in every detail, the same way Fish’s students found religious symbols in a list of names.

04The Guiding Premise

However, unlike Fish’s poem, the film had a guiding premise beyond just a list of names, making it easier to find interpretations of the story that unfolded. The red ball is the only object with color, which makes it stand out to the old man and the viewer. When the old man finally gets up and follows it, his figure becomes younger and younger and breaks out into a run. In parallel, color begins to return to the film, starting with the sky, then the ground and trees. Because these two changes happen together, the director links them. I did not have to invent that connection the way Fish’s students had to invent connections between “Jacobs” and Jacob’s ladder. Fish claims that “interpreters do not decode poems; they make them” (327), but my experience suggests it is not one or the other. I made the meaning that the return of color represents the vibrancy that comes with experiencing something new, but the film gave me the scenes to create that meaning. A list of names can become almost anything, but a black-and-white film where only a ball has color points the viewer in a certain direction.

05Who Brought the Direction?

Fish would likely answer that I brought the “direction” with me, too. Nothing in the film announces that returning color means vitality. I read it that way because of conventions I learned in high school English, where students across many schools read the same books and absorb the same habits, which may explain why so many viewers would reach the same conclusion. But even if the meaning of the color is learned, the change itself is not. Baxa still made color spread across the screen at the moment the old man grows younger, and any viewer has to account for that. Fish’s students had no such pattern to push against, since a list of names has no sequence of events, so their readings could go almost anywhere, while mine had to work around something happening. Fish is right that communities supply the strategies, but the text decides what those strategies have to work with.

06What I Brought

My own life also shaped my interpretation, even if I did not realize it at the time. Because my grandparents live so close to home, my experiences and moments with them shaped my interpretation significantly. Seeing my grandpa always smile and enjoy his time when we played cricket in the backyard contributed to my interpretation that the young figure represented the old man the whole time. This is why, in the film’s closing moments, I interpreted the sight of his inner youth playing with his physical self as evidence that his youth never left him (Baxa 2:33). It was still inside him, waiting for something to bring it out. Someone without that experience might have read the ending as sad, as if the young version was a memory he could never get back. This connects to Fish’s idea that readers interpret through what they bring with them (335). Still, not everything I brought was personal. Shared knowledge also shaped my understanding, such as the seasons representing stages of life: winter, which is bleak, and spring, which represents youth and prosperity. Like the connection of color to vitality, almost anyone who has been through school would recognize that, which is another example of Fish’s “interpretive community” (335). My reading came from both places at once, what I learned with everyone else and what I lived on my own.

07Catching His Breath

The moment the old man stops to catch his breath is where my reading depended most on what I brought to the film, yet the film still limited my options. Some viewers might read it as a reminder that the body cannot keep up with the spirit. However, the ball rolls back to him, giving him time to catch up (Baxa 1:05). I read this as the director suggesting that curiosity and joy are patient and do not have to disappear just because the body slows down. Looking back, I see that I chose hopeful reading partly because of my grandpa, which supports Fish’s claim that readers build meaning from their own experience. But I could only choose between these two readings because the film showed both the tired body and the returning ball. The text still set the limits of my choices.

08What I Didn’t See

Interpreting also meant ignoring things, and what I ignored shows how much I brought to the film. The black-and-white footage could have been plain nostalgia, the look of an old home movie, instead of a symbol of loneliness. The ball could have been a temptation pulling a tired man toward a chase he can’t win. The old man’s slowness could have simply been slowness. I never seriously considered any of these, because years of English classes taught me that a film like this should end in a hopeful lesson. The film allowed those other readings, but my habits ruled them out before I even noticed. That is Fish’s point at its sharpest: my interpretation felt like seeing, but it was also a series of quiet choices.

09The Space Between

My experience with “Ball” suggests that reading is neither pure discovery nor pure invention. I didn’t find a meaning that was sitting in the film, and I didn’t make one up from nothing. I built one from my classroom habits, my grandfather’s smile, and the images Baxa put on the screen. That has a practical consequence. When two readers disagree about a story, an essay, or even a news article, it’s less useful to ask who is right than to ask what each reader brought and what the text gave them. Fish taught me to notice my own assumptions, and the film taught me that those assumptions aren’t the whole story. Meaning happens in the space between a reader and a text, and understanding anything well means paying attention to both.
